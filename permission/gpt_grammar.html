How to use this guide

This guide is designed to be read like a book, but studied like a workbook. Each lesson page follows a repeatable rhythm: Core idea → examples → memory rules → common mistakes → quick practice.

1. Understand

Read the explanation once without trying to memorise every sentence. Identify the central rule.

2. Notice

Study the examples and ask what changes when the subject, time, number or meaning changes.

3. Apply

Do the quick practice without looking back at the rule.

4. Correct

Open the answer box. If you made an error, write the corrected rule in your own words.

Study rule: If you can explain why an answer is correct, you actually know the grammar. If you only recognise the answer, you have memorised it.

Nouns: meaning and identification

Core idea
A noun names a person, place, thing, substance, animal, activity or idea. In a sentence, nouns can perform different jobs: they can be subjects, objects or complements. A reliable identification test is to ask what the word refers to, then ask what role it plays in the sentence.

Examples

ExampleRina opened the window.
ExampleHonesty is a valuable quality.
ExampleThe children visited the museum.
ExampleWe bought rice for dinner.

Remember

  • Do not assume every noun is a physical object. Ideas such as honesty and happiness are nouns too.
  • A noun can be a name, a common word or a mass substance.

Core idea

Singular and plural nouns

Core idea
Plural nouns usually refer to more than one person, place, thing or idea. Most regular plurals are formed with -s or -es, but spelling changes and irregular forms must be learned as patterns. Always identify the singular form first, then choose the plural rule.

Examples

Examplebook → books
Examplebox → boxes
Examplebaby → babies
Examplechild → children
Examplemouse → mice

Remember

  • After many consonant + y endings, y changes to i before -es: city → cities.
  • Words ending in s, x, ch or sh often take -es: bus → buses.
  • Irregular plurals do not follow a single spelling rule: man → men, tooth → teeth.

Countable and uncountable nouns

Core idea
A countable noun names something that can be counted as individual units: one pen, two pens. An uncountable noun is treated as a mass, substance, concept or quantity that is not normally counted as separate units: water, advice, information. Countable nouns commonly take singular/plural forms; uncountable nouns normally do not take a plural in ordinary use.

Examples

ExampleI bought two notebooks.
ExampleWe need some information.
ExampleShe gave me a piece of advice.
ExampleThere are three bottles of water on the table.

Remember

  • Use how many with countable nouns: How many books?
  • Use how much with uncountable nouns: How much water?
  • Use a measure word when an uncountable quantity must be counted: a piece of advice, a bottle of oil.
  • Do not automatically add -s to words such as advice, information and furniture.

Adjective position

Core idea
An adjective describes or modifies a noun or pronoun. It can tell us about quality, size, colour, number, condition or another characteristic. Adjectives often appear before a noun, but they can also appear after a linking verb: a quiet room; the room is quiet.

Examples

Examplea bright student
Examplea large room
ExampleThe sky is cloudy.
ExampleThe children look happy.

Remember

  • Ask: What is the noun like?
  • After be, seem, look, feel and similar linking verbs, an adjective can describe the subject.
  • Do not confuse an adjective with an adverb: quick is an adjective; quickly is usually an adverb.

Degrees of comparison

Core idea
Adjectives can be used in three comparison patterns. The positive form describes without comparison. The comparative form compares two. The superlative form identifies one as highest or lowest within a group. Form choice depends on the adjective and on sentence meaning.

Examples

ExampleMina is tall.
ExampleMina is taller than Rupa.
ExampleMina is the tallest student in the group.
ExampleThis book is more useful than that one.

Remember

  • Comparatives normally use than when a direct comparison is stated.
  • Superlatives usually take the before them when they identify a member of a group.
  • Short adjectives often use -er/-est; longer adjectives often use more/most.

Conjunctions and linking ideas

Core idea
A conjunction links words, phrases or clauses. Coordinating conjunctions such as and, but, or and so connect ideas of equal status; other conjunctions introduce dependent clauses and express time, reason, condition, contrast or purpose.

Examples

ExampleRina studied and completed the task.
ExampleI wanted to go, but it was raining.
ExampleWe stayed home because the road was flooded.
ExampleCall me when you arrive.

Remember

  • Ask what relationship the linker expresses: addition, contrast, choice, result, reason or time.
  • Avoid joining complete sentences with only a comma.
  • Use the conjunction that matches the intended logic, not merely one that sounds familiar.

Core idea

The three time references: present, past, future

Core idea
Think of tense as a clock and aspect as a camera. Tense places an event in present, past or future time. Aspect shows whether we view the event as a routine, an action in progress, a completed action with present relevance, or an action continuing over a period.

Examples

ExamplePresent: She works here.
ExamplePast: She worked here last year.
ExampleFuture: She will work here next year.
ExampleContinuous: She is working now.
ExamplePerfect: She has finished the task.

Remember

  • First locate the time reference.
  • Then ask whether the action is routine, in progress, completed with relevance, or continuing over a duration.
  • Do not choose a tense only because of one time word. Meaning and context matter.

Present simple: forms

Core idea
The present simple is used for habits, routines, repeated actions, general truths and many stable states. Its action-verb form is the base verb for most subjects, with -s/-es in the third-person singular. In questions and negatives, do/does carries the tense and the main verb returns to the base form.

Examples

ExampleI walk to school.
ExampleShe walks to school.
ExampleThey do not play on Mondays.
ExampleDoes he live here?

Remember

  • He/she/it takes -s or -es in affirmative statements.
  • After does, use the base form: Does she work? not Does she works?
  • Use do not/does not for action verbs; be behaves differently.
